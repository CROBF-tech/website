If you run a small business, you have heard the pitch: you need to use AI. And in a way it is true. Between answering emails, building spreadsheets and taking care of customers, there is less time left than there should be. What almost nobody explains is where to start: which tasks make sense to hand over, how to ask for what you need, and which information you should never share. That is what this guide is for: owners and small teams without an IT department.

## 1. What an AI assistant is (and is not)

A chat-style AI assistant is a tool you talk to by writing, almost like messaging a person. In practice it is useful for three things: writing (drafts, emails, website copy), summarizing and sorting (long documents, messy notes) and helping you structure ideas (a plan, a comparison to think through a decision).

What it is not: a source of truth. AI assistants make mistakes, and sometimes they make them with total confidence: they will hand you a figure, a name or a detail that sounds perfectly credible and simply is not. They do not replace your judgment or your knowledge of the business either. What they save you is mechanical work, not decisions. And be careful with anything current: several popular assistants do search the web today, but not all of them, and not every time. Unless the tool says it is searching the internet, do not assume what it tells you is up to date; and when it does search, check the sources it cites before taking anything as fact.

A useful way to think about it: it is a very fast collaborator and a very good writer who, every now and then, gets something wrong with a perfectly straight face. Extremely valuable, if you supervise it.

## 2. Tasks where it usually pays off

In small businesses, the payoff shows up in a short list of tasks:

- **Emails and customer replies.** Paste the inquiry with some context and you get a draft to adjust. The saving is not the perfect email; it is not starting from a blank page.
- **Summarizing long documents.** A forty-page PDF, a set of tender specifications, a report: in minutes you have the key points and know where to focus. The real risk is that the summary skips something important, so validating the summary is not enough: in tenders and contracts, always check the critical points (deadlines, penalties, obligations, amounts) in the original.
- **Spreadsheets and formulas.** Describe what you want to calculate and how your data is laid out, and the assistant proposes the formula or shows you how to build it. No more googling formulas blind.
- **Product descriptions.** Hand over a list of features and you get text for the website or catalog, in a few tones and lengths to pick from.
- **Meeting preparation.** Drop in the notes from your last meeting and it returns an agenda, a follow-up list, even a draft of the minutes.

In none of these does the assistant "do the work": it clears the mechanical part so you can focus on the part that needs judgment.

## 3. How to ask well

The difference between a useless answer and one that saves you half an hour usually lies in the request, not in the tool. Four things change everything:

1. **Context.** Who you are, what the business is, who the text is for. "I run a neighborhood hardware store" changes everything that comes after it.
2. **Examples.** If you have a text you like, paste it as a reference. "Write something like this" beats any abstract instruction.
3. **Expected format.** How long it should be, list or paragraph, formal or plain tone, one version or three options to choose from.
4. **Iteration.** The first answer is rarely the final one. Ask for adjustments: "shorter," "more direct," "less salesy." It is a conversation, not a one-shot bet.

A bad request:

> "Write me an email for a customer."

A good request:

> "I run a hardware store. A customer wrote this: [paste the inquiry]. I want to reply that the part will take a week to arrive and that we can place a pre-order for them. Draft the reply: friendly tone, under 120 words, no promises I cannot keep. Here is an email I sent before so you can match the tone: [paste the example]."

Notice the difference: the second gives the assistant everything it needs, and leaves you with a draft that is almost ready to send.

## 4. What you should not share

Now the careful part, and it is not negotiable. Simple rule: if you would not want that information to end up somewhere you do not control, do not give it to the assistant.

- **Passwords and credentials.** Never, not even once: no banking logins, no system access, no verification codes.
- **Customers' personal data.** Names with ID numbers, phones, addresses, billing details. If you need help with a text, swap real data for "Customer A" or initials. Beyond caution, there is a legal reason too: laws such as Argentina's Personal Data Protection Law 25.326, and similar laws elsewhere, require you to protect your customers' data and, as a rule, not hand it to third parties without their consent.
- **Sensitive financial information.** Balances, card details, internal figures you would rather keep private.
- **Confidential contracts.** Before asking it to summarize one, check what your own confidentiality clause says. Often it is enough to strip out names, amounts and brands.

Two habits worth having from day one: review the tool's privacy and data-use settings (look for where it explains how your data is used), and, if you can, work from a business account rather than a personal one. Not every tool offers the same options here, so check the one you choose before handing it real information.

## 5. Always review

The assistant can write, but it does not sign anything. Everything it produces goes through a human before it leaves:

- One person checks figures, prices and dates against your records, not against memory.
- Customer, product and supplier names get verified against real data.
- Nothing reaches a customer without a full read: no email, no quote, no published description.

In a small team, assign the responsibility by name: "Marta signs off on this." When review is everyone's job, it is nobody's.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Trusting it with everything right away.** Start with low-risk tasks (internal drafts, summaries) and only then move to sensitive work, always with review.
- **Accepting it as is.** If the text is 80% right, the risk lies in publishing the 20% that is wrong. Review is not optional.
- **Asking too little.** "Do marketing for my business" is impossible to answer well. Vague request, vague answer.
- **Sharing sensitive information out of convenience.** "It is just this once" is exactly the phrase you will remember when something goes wrong.
- **Delegating decisions that are yours.** Pricing, hiring, legal matters: the assistant helps you organize the analysis, but the decision is yours.

## 7. A realistic starting point for this week

You do not need to transform the company in seven days. You need to start with something concrete:

- [ ] Pick one repetitive task that is eating your time (customer replies are usually a good candidate).
- [ ] Try the assistant on that task only, using detailed requests like the ones in section 3.
- [ ] Decide who reviews the output, and say it out loud to the team.
- [ ] Write a short list of data you never share (passwords, customer data, financial information) and hand it to the whole team.
- [ ] Check the tool's privacy settings and decide whether you will use a business account.
- [ ] On Friday, ask yourself the honest question: did this save me time or create extra work? Let that answer decide whether you keep going, adjust, or stop.

None of this will turn your business upside down overnight. What an AI assistant can do, used well and reviewed with care, is give you back part of your day that you are currently losing to mechanical tasks. In a small company, that time is not a small thing. Start small, review everything, and adjust as you go.
