Almost every small business knows it should be backing up. Very few have a plan that survives past the third month.

The problem is almost never technology. It's how the habit is designed. A backup that depends on someone remembering, or one so complex nobody understands it, ends up abandoned. And an abandoned plan is worse than none, because it makes you feel protected when you're not.

The good news: a sustainable backup plan doesn't need sophisticated infrastructure. It needs clear decisions, made once, automated forever. Here's a practical guide.

## 1. Start with what hurts

Before picking tools, do an honest inventory: which data, if it vanished tomorrow, would stop the business?

For most small businesses, the list looks like this:

- Invoicing and accounting
- Client documents and contracts
- Email and contacts
- Shared work files (network folders or cloud)
- Passwords and access credentials (no, a notebook doesn't count as a backup)
- The website and, if you sell online, the store

Then sort into two or three tiers: critical (backed up daily), important (weekly), and "could be recreated" (monthly or not at all). The fewer categories, the higher the odds the plan actually happens.

One detail almost always missing: store the plan itself somewhere else, not in the same "backups" folder. A plan that lives only on the server it backs up isn't a plan.

## 2. The 3-2-1 rule, translated for small business

The classic rule says: three copies, on two different media, one off-site. It sounds like data center jargon, but in practice it's simple:

- **Copy 1:** the live file, where you already work (your cloud folder, your invoicing system).
- **Copy 2:** an automatic backup to a different destination. An external drive at the office, or another cloud account.
- **Copy 3:** something outside the building and outside the main account. A drive at a partner's home, a different cloud provider, whatever works.

The strength of the rule is the third copy. Modern ransomware actively hunts for backups connected to the network. If your whole plan lives in one account, a single compromise leaves you at zero.

## 3. Automate it, or accept it won't happen

Here's the most common mistake, and I say it fondly because I made it myself: **manual backups don't exist long-term.** They work for three months, break during vacation, resume in September, die in November.

Minimum automation checklist:

- [ ] The backup runs on its own, nobody triggers it
- [ ] It notifies on failure (an email or message; silence is not confirmation)
- [ ] The destination isn't the same account as the source
- [ ] At least one copy keeps history, not just "the latest"
- [ ] Someone knows where everything is, and that someone isn't only the owner

That last point deserves its own paragraph. If you're the only one who understands the backups, your plan has a single point of failure with legs. Document on one simple page: what's backed up, how often, where it lands, how to restore. One page. Not a treatise.

## 4. Test the restore, not the backup

A backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis, not a plan. Twice a year, book an hour to:

1. Pick a random file from each tier (critical, important, the rest)
2. Restore it from the backup, not the original
3. Open it and check it's the version you expected
4. Note how long it took

That last data point is gold: in a real incident, it tells you whether you're talking about an afternoon or a week of downtime. And if something fails in the test, better to find out on a calm Tuesday than on a Friday with the system down.

## 5. Common mistakes (the hall of shame)

- **Backing up everything equally.** If everything is critical, nothing is, and the cost of storing everything forever ends up killing the plan.
- **Relying on one person's good memory.** Memory doesn't scale, doesn't document itself and doesn't sign contracts.
- **Storing backups in the same cloud as the originals, in another folder.** Same basket.
- **Never testing a restore.** The day you need it is not the day to discover the file has been corrupted for eight months.
- **Trusting the "version history" of a single tool.** It helps, but it doesn't replace an independent copy.

## 6. A realistic starting point

If you have nothing today, don't aim for the perfect plan. Aim for this one, in an afternoon:

1. List what's critical (half an hour, seriously)
2. Turn on version history in your current cloud
3. Add a second automatic copy to a different destination
4. Send a drive with the essentials somewhere else
5. Write the one-page documentation
6. Set a reminder every six months to test restores

Done. That's already more than most small businesses, and it's a plan one person can maintain.

## The takeaway

The goal of a backup plan isn't having backups: it's being able to keep working the day after the disaster. Everything else (the tools, the acronyms, the rules) is there to serve that.

A good plan shows in one signal: it happens without anyone thinking about it. If your current backup needs a hero, reminders or faith, it's not a plan. It's a bet. And bets, in security, are lost exactly on the day they matter most.
