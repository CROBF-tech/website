**Your new developer should never have to guess how to start.**

If someone joins the team and ships a first PR in week one, understands how you work, and knows exactly who to ask, your onboarding works. If not, the problem isn't the newcomer: it's the process.

The good news is that onboarding can be designed just like software: with intention, clear stages, and iteration. Here are the pieces that make the biggest difference in helping someone become productive and feel part of the team from week one.

## 🚀 Before Day 1: Have Everything Ready

Day 1 starts long before day 1. If your new hire spends three days waiting for an access request, you lose momentum and trust.

- **Set up all access:** repos, tools, messaging, environments. Everything working before they walk through the door.
- **Get the environment ready:** a configured machine, or clear instructions on what to install.
- **Make sure the setup README actually works:** run it with someone who knows nothing about the project. If it has steps nobody ever executed, that's not documentation — that's fiction.
- **Build the first-week agenda:** who they'll meet, what they'll read, what they'll do. There will always be surprises, but the first one shouldn't be "nothing was assigned".

## 🤝 Assign a Buddy from Minute One

No question should go unanswered because someone "didn't know who to bother".

- **Pick a specific person**, not "the team". Teams don't answer questions; people do.
- **Block time on the buddy's calendar:** supporting someone isn't a spare-time activity.
- **Tell the whole team** that the buddy is the go-to person for the small questions.
- **Separate the roles:** the buddy supports the day-to-day; management and evaluation stay with the lead.

## 🔨 A Small First PR in Week One

Nothing builds belonging like contributing. And nothing isolates like waiting weeks "to be ready".

- **Pick a real but small task:** a minor fix, a clear improvement — visible impact, low risk.
- **Goal: first PR shipped within the first five days.** It doesn't matter that it's small; what matters is going through the full workflow end to end.
- **Review the code generously:** explain the why, not just the what.

## 📚 Living Documentation, Not a Museum

- Newcomers are the best broken-docs detectors: if something is confusing, that's a signal.
- For every "I got stuck here", suggest a small PR to the documentation.
- Make it part of the process: whoever uses the docs improves them. Documentation stays fresh, and the new developer leaves a mark from the very start.

## 🗺️ A 30-Day Plan with Clear Expectations

"Learn a lot" is not a plan. A plan states what's expected and when.

- **Week 1:** environment running, first PR, meet the team.
- **Weeks 2 and 3:** real tasks with support, understanding the domain.
- **Week 4:** independent contributions and something bigger than a tiny PR.
- **Write the expectations down and share them:** mystery is the worst manager.

## 🔄 Frequent Check-Ins and Two-Way Feedback

- **Short and frequent beats long and rare.** Fifteen minutes a week is pure gold.
- **Ask concrete questions:** what slowed you down this week? What was missing?
- **Give feedback yourself too:** new developers shouldn't have to guess how you see them.
- **Listen in the other direction:** the onboarding itself improves with what people tell you. That also teaches the newcomer that this is a place where people listen.

## ⚠️ The Common Mistakes That Sink It All

- **Throwing the newcomer at an outdated wiki.** Beyond frustrating, it sends a very clear message: "nobody here maintains anything".
- **Leaving them alone to "figure it out".** That's not autonomy; it's abandonment with an excuse.
- **Overloading them with meetings.** If the first week is a calendar marathon, when do they write code?
- **Expecting the output of someone with years of context.** Full productivity takes time; the goal of the first weeks is learning fast with support, not sprinting from day one.

## ✅ Conclusion

Great onboarding isn't a fancy welcome event: it's a system. Access ready, a buddy present, an early first PR, living docs, a 30-day plan, and feedback flowing both ways. With all of that in place, the new developer doesn't just perform sooner — they stay, they contribute, and they make the team stronger.
